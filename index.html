Part 1 · The plumbing

What happens when you type a web address

Marketers don't need to be engineers. But when a site goes down, loads slowly, or shows a "Not secure" warning, customers leave, and you need to know who to call. Start with the address itself.

Try it: take apart a URL

A URL (Uniform Resource Locator) is the full address of one page. The domain name (dashed box) is just the part a business registers. Click each piece.

Who rents you the name

Registrar

A company that manages the leasing of domain names. You register a name and it's reserved for you as long as you keep paying, usually yearly. GoDaddy is the largest; Namecheap, Squarespace Domains and Cloudflare are others.

Who stores the site

Server

An always-on computer that answers requests for a website's files. Big sites run on thousands of servers in server farms (data centers). Servers make so much heat that Facebook built one data center about 70 miles from the Arctic Circle so cold outside air can cool it.

Try it: follow a page request

Computers find each other by number (an IP address), not by name. The DNS (Domain Name System) works like the internet's phone book. This is simplified; the real process has more steps.

Web browsers

A browser translates the web's language (HTML) into a page a person can read and click. Browsers interpret code slightly differently, so a checkout that works in Chrome can break in Safari. If every Safari user suddenly can't buy, revenue drops, so marketers test their key pages in more than one browser.

Google ChromeApple SafariMicrosoft EdgeFirefoxSamsung InternetOpera

Chrome accounts for roughly two of every three page views worldwide, and Safari is a distant second, mostly because of iPhones (StatCounter). Most of your customers will be on one of those two.

Web hosting: rent or buy the server?

Rent (web hosting)

A host rents you a small slice of a server. Your site is always on, and you don't need to buy or maintain hardware. This is where nearly every small business starts.

Own or lease whole servers

When a site grows very large or gets huge traffic, it upgrades its hosting plan or runs its own servers, often on cloud platforms such as Amazon Web Services.

Try it: what can a snoop see?

Data sent over the internet isn't private by default. A security certificate turns on encryption (TLS, the newer version of SSL). Switch between HTTP and HTTPS to see what someone on the same café Wi-Fi could read.

Content management systems

WordPress

Runs over 40% of all websites. Free and flexible, and you can edit the code directly if you need to.

Wix & Squarespace

Drag-and-drop builders with hosting included. Fastest way to get a good-looking small-business site.

Shopify

A CMS built for selling: product pages, cart, payments and inventory in one system.

Web analytics

Analytics reports what people do on your site: where they came from, what they looked at, and what they bought. Chapter 3 covers it in depth. For now, imagine you own an online store. What would you want to know? Sort your answers into three buckets, and you've built the success formula below.

What changed since the textbook
  • Since 2018, Chrome and other browsers label every plain-HTTP page "Not secure", and Google gives HTTPS pages a small ranking boost. Today every page should be HTTPS, not just checkout.
  • People still say "SSL certificate," but the encryption is now TLS. Certificates are free through Let's Encrypt and most hosts, so cost is no longer an excuse.
  • Google Analytics 4 replaced Universal Analytics in July 2023, and in 2024 it renamed "conversions" to key events. The idea in this chapter is the same.

Defining success

Conversions by type of website

A conversion is the action the website most wants a visitor to take. Each type of site has a primary conversion and some secondary ones that lead toward it. Pick a type.

Lecture case

Analyzing the success of RogueFitness.com

Rogue Fitness makes CrossFit-style equipment (barbells, weight plates, squat racks) and also sells shoes and apparel. It has two main target customers: owners of CrossFit-style gyms and individual athletes equipping a garage gym. We'll walk through all three parts of the formula. Open roguefitness.com in another tab and follow along; the site has changed since the textbook screenshots (February 2023).

1 · Traffic

One month of sessions by source. These numbers are made up for teaching, not Rogue's real data. Group them different ways.

Why is direct traffic so high?

Rogue is a famous name among CrossFit athletes. Anyone shopping for equipment goes straight to the site, and people who first came from an ad or email come back directly next time. Direct traffic costs nothing, so it's very profitable.

Is 46,667 paid-referral visits good?

You can't tell from this alone. It's a lot of visits, but whether they were bought at a profit depends on ad cost, conversion rate and margin. That's why traffic is only one part of the formula.

Search: check it yourself

Google "barbell," "squat rack," "weightlifting shoes." For each: Is there a Rogue ad? Does Rogue appear in the organic results, and in what position? Are there good searches where Rogue doesn't appear?

Channels overlap

A search ad on a term where Rogue already ranks first may just move a visit from unpaid to paid. An email may only speed up a visit that would have happened directly. More traffic in one channel isn't always new traffic.

2 · Conversion: discovery → evaluation → payment

All that traffic only pays off if the website helps people buy. A shopper goes through three stages. Click each.

3 · Revenue per conversion

Do: recommend add-ons

"Often bought with" suggestions (collars, chalk, a bar rack) raise the order size. The tactic is simple; good recommendations take sophisticated software.

Do: offer customization

Rogue's custom option lets a gym put its name on a barbell. In the textbook example, the price rose from $350 to $425, about 21% more revenue from the same conversion.

Don't: push expensive items first

Featuring only high-priced products or sorting categories from highest to lowest price raises order size but makes products harder to find. Conversion rate usually matters more to profit than order size, so discovery comes first.

Apply it

Assignment: evaluate a local business website

Pick a local business with a website (a restaurant, dentist, gym, church or shop) and run the three-part test. Notice the order: you define the conversion first, because local businesses vary so much.
